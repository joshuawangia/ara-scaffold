## T1 — Safety failure suffices for obligatory retraction

**Proposed thesis.** When a later assessor discovers that the original belief in “might p” fails worldly-information safety, the original speaker is thereby obliged to retract that very might-assertion, holding its use context fixed.

**Verdict: partially anticipated.** The safety side, assessment side, and withdrawal motivations have precedents; the asserted sufficiency implication joining them was not established in the read passages. Calling the entire proposal unprecedented would conceal these substantial antecedents.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Beddor–Goldstein (2021), PDF5 and23–24: safety constrains modal knowledge, with truth and belief separately stated. PDF2 supplies a retraction motivation and PDF9 invokes assessor information; neither passage identifies safety failure with the falsity of the original bare might-claim.
- Hawke (2024, Stable), §5.2 and§6.1–6.2, PDF14–19: compares safety semantics with indefeasibility and gives conditions for denying knowledge. This is closer to withdrawing a knowledge attribution than retracting the embedded might-assertion.
- Ask Zaar (online2025/issue2026), PDF1 and10–11: argues that requiring safety need not entail contextualism. This is a general constraint on the inference, not a treatment of worldly-information safety or retrospective retraction.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q01](#q01), [Q03](#q03), [Q08](#q08), [Q18](#q18).

**What a later route must supply.** Specify the assertion being retracted, whose belief is unsafe, and when the belief is evaluated. Supply a further argument from safety failure to the applicable retraction norm. Loss of knowledge that might p does not by itself establish falsity of might p.

**Search mapping:** N01, N02, N03, N09, P0, P4.

## T2 — Safety is the exact criterion: necessity and sufficiency

**Proposed thesis.** For the same earlier might-assertion and fixed use context, obligatory retraction at a later assessment context occurs if and only if its original belief fails worldly-information safety as evaluated there. In particular, safety preservation rules out an obligation to retract.

**Verdict: not found.** No defense of this exact necessity-and-sufficiency claim was located in the bounded 2018–2026 search and inspected texts. This records absence of located support, not novelty, truth, feasibility, or a certified open problem.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Beddor–Goldstein (2021), PDF23–24: distinguishes the nearby-world safety condition from the truth conjunct in its knowledge analysis. No equivalence with a retraction norm is given there.
- Hawke (2024, Expressivists), Appendix A, PDF29–30: deliberately removes the truth conjunct in the safety variant he tests. This illustrates why truth and safety must not be silently conflated; his countermodels are not a proof about retraction.
- MacFarlane (2014), printed108–109: states a sufficient trigger for required retraction and explicitly permits retracting something still thought true. The latter defeats an unrestricted claim about all permissible retractions, but is not by itself a counterexample to the narrower biconditional about obligations.
- Rudin (online2024/issue2025), PDF7–9: rejection judgments can diverge from falsity judgments. Rejection is not the original speaker’s retrospective retraction obligation.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q03](#q03), [Q04](#q04), [Q10](#q10), [Q14](#q14).

**What a later route must supply.** Test both directions separately against the actual truth conjunct and an explicit norm. Do not infer necessity by reversing MacFarlane’s one-way rule; do not equate permissibility, required retraction, actual retraction, and another speaker’s rejection.

**Search mapping:** N01, N03, N09, N10, P0, P1.

## T3 — Asymmetric loss explains retraction without relativism

**Proposed thesis.** Retraction of a knowledge attribution can be explained by stakes-dependent estimates of safety, reliability, or evidential probability, without requiring assessment-relative truth or a changed knowledge threshold.

**Verdict: anticipated.** This rival explanatory thesis is explicitly developed by Dinges. Its application to knowledge-attribution judgments should be cited as prior work; it is not new merely because it was absent from the frozen corpus.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Dinges (2021), §4.1 and§4.3.1, PDF5–6 and10–11: changing loss functions bias estimates of an uncertain epistemic quantity, changing willingness to stand by a knowledge claim. Footnote10 expressly extends the proposal to safety and reliability. The account is an error theory consistent with denying contextualism, relativism, and pragmatic encroachment.
- Ask Zaar (online2025/issue2026), PDF1 and10–11 supplies an independent reason not to infer contextualism just from accepting a safety condition; it does not supply Dinges’s psychological mechanism.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q05](#q05), [Q06](#q06), [Q07](#q07), [Q18](#q18).

**What a later route must supply.** Any extension to bare epistemic modals or to a normative obligation must be argued separately. A behavioral explanation is not a demonstration that the relevant retractions are required or that the underlying truth value changes.

**Search mapping:** N05, N01, P0.

## T4 — Stable acceptance replaces safety and guides withdrawal

**Proposed thesis.** Use stability under the relevant available evidence to assess modal knowledge; when later information exposes instability, require retraction of the earlier knowledge-ascription under a fixed use context.

**Verdict: partially anticipated.** Stability, available evidence, and denial of modal knowledge are anticipated. The fixed-use retrospective obligation added in this thesis remains unverified. A fork proposing only stable acceptance or denial on evidential defeat should instead be marked anticipated.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Hawke (2023), §5, PDF7–10: formally models accessible refinements bounded by worldly information. Hawke (2024, Stable), §6, PDF16–21, develops the journal treatment using total available evidence and explicit acceptance/rejection clauses.
- The2024 paper, PDF19, says a speaker who can assert that total available evidence does not support the content should deny the knowledge attribution. PDF18 distinguishes relevant availability from evidence that can merely be collected; PDF22 leaves its multi-agent/contextual treatment for further work.
- The2023 paper is a distinct extended abstract, not a substitute for the2024 paper. The latter’s rejection condition supplies a major part of the proposed route, but does not itself track a particular earlier assertion across contexts or state MacFarlane’s retraction norm.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q08](#q08), [Q09](#q09).

**What a later route must supply.** Give the missing link from current denial to retraction of the earlier token. Define availability across agents and times; do not treat all later-acquirable information as admissible defeat.

**Search mapping:** N04, N02, P2, prior Mighty Knowledge citation set.

## T5 — Make worldly information assessment-relative

**Proposed thesis.** Interpret worldly probability through the later assessor’s credences, or index the worldly-information selector to the assessment context, so that safety itself changes across assessments and explains retraction.

**Verdict: partially anticipated.** The assessor-credence interpretation is explicit prior work. Replacing a use-context parameter with a later assessment parameter, and deriving a retraction obligation, are additional commitments not licensed by that passage.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Beddor–Goldstein (2021), §6.2, PDF28–29: explicitly allows provisionally revised assessor credences as one interpretation of contextual probability. This is an option among several, not an identification of all worldly information with an assessor’s actual information.
- Hawke (2024, Stable), §7.1, PDF22: considers contextualist versus subject-sensitive treatments of availability and leaves the choice unsettled.
- Ask Zaar (online2025/issue2026), PDF10–11: defends the possibility of an invariant core to similarity judgments. Changing an ascriber’s judgments does not automatically change semantic safety.
- Xu (2020), publisher abstract only in prior L3 receipts: proposes probabilistic safety from the perspective of ascribers. The full text remains unavailable; its treatment of later assessment and retraction is unknown.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q02](#q02), [Q09](#q09), [Q18](#q18).

**What a later route must supply.** State which parameter is fixed at use and which varies at assessment; distinguish nearby-world information from actual assessor evidence. Show that the move preserves the intended safety/knowledge logic and yields the claimed norm.

**Search mapping:** N02, N09, P0, P4.

## T6 — Flexible truth assessment with current-context retraction

**Proposed thesis.** Allow the QUD to select which context is relevant to a truth-value judgment while treating retraction as publicly ceasing to accept, or to advocate acceptance of, the earlier content from one’s current context.

**Verdict: anticipated.** This exact distinction is already proposed by Beddor–Egan. Neither QUD-sensitive truth judgments nor the current-context retraction account should be presented as new. This verdict attributes a view, not a consensus.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Beddor–Egan (2018), §4–5, PDF17–18: explicitly distinguishes flexible truth assessments from current-context acceptance/rejection/retraction; footnote16 discusses and rejects an alternative that would make those attitudes QUD-flexible too. The experiments manipulate the assessment QUD for an earlier utterance.
- Rudin (online2024/issue2025), §2.1–2.3, PDF7–9 and discussion PDF38, offers a different treatment of truth versus update/rejection; it is not evidence that the flexible-relativist explanation is settled.
- Skovgaard-Olsen–Cantwell (2023) remains abstract-only: reported truth-value shifts were not matched by strong average support for the retraction norm. Marques (2024) remains unavailable. Their missing full texts limit the empirical assessment, not the attribution of the2018 proposal.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q12](#q12), [Q13](#q13), [Q14](#q14).

**What a later route must supply.** If the new claim is that QUD selects worldly information for safety, that is an extra link to be evaluated under T5. Do not attribute arbitrary QUD-sensitive retraction to the2018 account.

**Search mapping:** N06, N08, P1.

## T7 — Explain withdrawal through speech-act effects

**Proposed thesis.** Explain retraction by cancellation or proposed cancellation of the earlier act’s normative/update effects, leaving room for a distinction between truth conditions and conversational acceptance rather than deriving every withdrawal from false content.

**Verdict: anticipated.** The speech-act rival is directly anticipated in recent full texts; differences between cancellation and proposals requiring uptake matter. Its general idea also predates the2018 window in MacFarlane’s own discussion.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Caponetto (2024), author preprint PDF1 and§2–3: develops retraction as cancellation of a prior act’s normative effects, with felicity conditions and audience roles.
- Bussière-Caraes et al. (2024), §7.3–7.4, PDF9 and15–20: refines the account into proposals to undo changes, subject to uptake and dependencies among subsequent updates. These authors do not simply give speakers unilateral power to erase an utterance.
- Rudin (online2024/issue2025), §2 and subsequent formal model: separates truth-conditional semantics from update acceptance. Its discussion of rejection is a complementary piece, not a worked-out account of retrospective retraction.
- Beddor–Goldstein (2021), PDF2: already motivates retracting a modal assertion by no longer standing behind its update.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q14](#q14), [Q15](#q15), [Q16](#q16).

**What a later route must supply.** To create a new L3 result, specify what safety contributes beyond these existing pragmatic accounts and why that contribution yields a duty rather than merely a possible or successful discourse move.

**Search mapping:** N07, P1, P3.

## T8 — Later retraction helps determine original content

**Proposed thesis.** Explain some retraction cases by temporal externalism: later responses to correction partly determine the modal base/content of the earlier utterance, instead of changing the truth of an already fixed content at a new assessment context.

**Verdict: anticipated.** Ball already argues for this rival. A fork that merely says later retraction can help fix what the earlier modal utterance meant should cite Ball rather than claim novelty.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Ball (2020), PDF1, §2 and§3 (especially PDF31–33): explicitly develops temporal externalism and applies it to epistemic-modal retraction. Subsequent conduct can help fix earlier content where prior intentions and dispositions did not settle it.
- Brendel (2019) and Kuzmanović (2023) are additional abstract-only contextualist retraction leads. Their disagreement over indexical/nonindexical approaches is not evidence for Ball’s particular metasemantic thesis, and their full arguments were not obtained.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q17](#q17).

**What a later route must supply.** This changes the explanatory target relative to a fixed-use assessment theory. It is not a derivation of MacFarlane’s norm from worldly safety, and no safety-based elaboration is established by Ball’s general claim.

**Search mapping:** N08, P1, prior MacFarlane2014 citation set.

## T9 — Fragmented, topic-sensitive knowledge as a safety-free rival

**Proposed thesis.** Replace the safety analysis of modal knowledge with topic-sensitive, fragmented acceptance states and contextual alternatives, then use that framework to explain why an earlier modal commitment must be withdrawn.

**Verdict: partially anticipated.** The semantic rival is substantially anticipated. The addition of a fixed-use retrospective retraction obligation is not established in the inspected sections. Neither mere co-citation of MacFarlane nor a formal knowledge-rejection condition supplies it.

**Closest sources and what they establish:**

- Hawke (2024, Expressivists), §§5–6 and Appendices A–B, PDF19–32: develops Fragmented and Topic-sensitive Expressivism (FaTE), a contextualist refinement, and modal Gettier treatments. Appendix A assesses a safety variant without the truth conjunct; its logical criticisms must be read with that restriction.
- Hawke (2025), Fragmentation and expression, abstract and its formal development: combines topical fragmentation with semantic expressivism, addressing connective behavior, logical omniscience and epistemic must. It is a substantive later successor, not a retraction derivation.
- Holliday–Mandelkern (2024), PDF58: explicitly lists truth, correctness, agreement and retraction as further questions for its possibility-semantic framework. This reinforces the need to distinguish a knowledge logic from a completed pragmatics.

**Page-checked quotations:** [Q10](#q10), [Q11](#q11), [Q19](#q19), [Q20](#q20).

**What a later route must supply.** A route proposing only fragmentation/topic sensitivity as an alternative to safety is anticipated by Hawke. A stronger retraction route must state and defend its bridge explicitly, with the target framework and factivity assumptions held constant.

**Search mapping:** N04, N07, N09, prior Hawke citation sets.

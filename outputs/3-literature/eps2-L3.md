# eps2 L3 followup: worldly-information safety and assessment-sensitive retraction

## Verdict

**Partial.** The exact requested integration was **not located in the accessible full texts**. That narrower negative finding is not a claim that it is absent from the literature, still open, or novel. Eight verified PDFs were added; none of the eight previously unavailable modern shortlist papers was recovered.

There is more prior engagement than the earlier `unknown` summary suggested. Beddor–Goldstein’s own *Mighty Knowledge* discusses retraction, rejects a modal-knowledge claim using assessor information, explicitly cites MacFarlane2014, and allows assessor credences as one interpretation of contextual probability. Dinges’s *Knowledge and Asymmetric Loss* offers a developed connection between estimates of safety-type properties and retraction behavior, while denying that relativism is required. These make **partial** a better verdict than a blanket “not found.”

For **addressed**, I would require a read account explicitly connecting (a) worldly-information safety for modal knowledge, (b) evaluation of the same earlier utterance with its use context fixed and its assessment context changed, and (c) the resulting obligation to retract. Co-citation, generic loss of knowledge, contextual variation in a safety estimate, or simply conjoining separate theories does not establish that connection.

The remaining issue is precise: how does the information used to evaluate safety at nearby worlds relate to the information supplied by a later assessment context, and what licenses the retraction obligation? This is an analyst’s description of what the read evidence has not supplied, not a new theorem or a certified open problem.

The two information roles should not be silently identified. In Beddor–Goldstein, the safety operator evaluates at nearby worlds paired with their worldly information (§5.3.3); the truth condition is separately stated (§5.4). Their contextual interpretation selects worldly information through a contextually fixed function (§6.2), and provisionally revised assessor credences are only one option for interpreting its probability measure (PDF29). MacFarlane’s Retraction Rule instead evaluates the earlier assertion as used at its original context and assessed from the later one. The read texts do not show that safety failure is necessary or sufficient for that retraction obligation.

*Mighty Knowledge* PDF2 gives a pragmatic motivation for withdrawing an update; it does not derive MacFarlane’s norm. The word “assessor” on PDF29 therefore strengthens a partial connection without completing it. This distinction is why the verdict is not “addressed.”

## A strong rival explanation

Dinges2021 is the strongest newly acquired neighboring paper. It discusses retraction of knowledge ascriptions (PDF3–4), contrasts threshold-shifting approaches with its own account (PDF5–6), and develops an asymmetric-loss explanation of retraction judgments (§4.3.1, PDF10–11). Footnote10 explicitly extends the discussion to estimates of safety or reliability. Its abstract states that the account is consistent with rejecting contextualism, relativism and pragmatic encroachment.

That gives a safety-related account of retraction behavior, but it is not Beddor–Goldstein’s worldly-information semantics and does not establish assessment-sensitive truth. It is evidence against treating a behavioral safety–retraction correlation as sufficient proof of L3’s stronger semantic/normative claim.

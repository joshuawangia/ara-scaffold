# From Safety to Retraction: What ARA’s Unclosed T2 Test Does—and Does Not—Show

*Candidate gap-T2-analysis · Analysis of ARA output · Not an ARA-derived route · 30 October 2026*

## Abstract

Does failure of worldly-information safety require a speaker to retract an earlier epistemic-modal assertion? A stronger proposal, T2, makes such failure both necessary and sufficient for obligatory retraction while holding the original use context fixed. ARA tested this proposal and its two directions against a frozen representation of the literature. Its focused test returned six OPEN routes and no accepted inference. This paper explains that outcome without treating unsuccessful search as a refutation. A necessary condition on knowledge does not itself connect an unsafe belief to a duty concerning a particular assertion. A one-way retraction rule does not establish that its trigger exhausts all relevant obligations. The literature already supplies substantial parts of the surrounding debate; a bounded dossier classified only the exact biconditional as not found within its search scope. The resulting task is to supply and defend the missing connections, with belief, assertion, truth, assessment and obligation kept distinct. A new construction experiment, gap2, is in progress at this paper’s evidence checkpoint. No result from that unfinished run is credited.

## 1. The question and the status of this paper

An earlier assertion that something might be the case can become difficult to stand behind after further information arrives. Several explanations compete: the assertion may be false from a later assessment context; the speaker may lose knowledge; the conversational update may no longer be acceptable; or later responses may help determine what was originally said. These explanations do not identify the same event or impose the same norm. L3 is the project’s label for a proposed connection between worldly-information safety in an account of modal knowledge and an assessment-sensitive obligation to retract. It is a research question, not a result established by naming both literatures. [1, 2, 14]

The candidate T2 strengthens that connection. For a particular earlier might-assertion, with its original use context held fixed, a later assessment requires retraction if and only if the associated original belief fails worldly-information safety as evaluated there. Thus unsafe belief would suffice for obligation, and safety preservation would exclude obligation. The thesis comes from the literature analyst’s dossier. It was supplied to ARA as a disclosed external hypothesis, not discovered by its search and not added as a premise. [14, 15]

This paper is likewise an external analysis of the recorded output. It is not a closed ARA argument, a native essay export, or an independently audited proof of T2. Its contribution is to locate the inferential work that the recorded test left undone and to explain which future results would address it. No substantive novel philosophical theorem or literature priority is claimed. The operator has authorized drafting all candidates while reserving final submission selection; this document does not choose among them. [17]

## 2. Four distinctions the proposed bridge must preserve

First, a belief that might p, an assertion of might p, and a claim to know that might p are different objects. Beddor and Goldstein’s information-sensitive safety condition constrains when a belief amounts to knowledge. In their formulation, nearby worlds at which the belief is held must make it true relative to the worldly information there. Their later presentation separately states belief, truth and safety conditions for probabilistic knowledge. A claim about loss of knowledge therefore cannot be silently transferred to the truth of the embedded modal assertion. [1, PDF pp. 5, 23–24]

Second, the context of the earlier assertion must remain identifiable. MacFarlane’s Retraction Rule concerns an unretracted assertion made in one context and evaluated from another. The rule requires retraction when its content is not true as used at the original context and assessed from the later one. Changing the content, the original use context, or the object being withdrawn changes the proposal under examination. [2, printed pp. 108–109; PDF pp. 122–123]

Third, permission to reject, an obligation to retract, an actual retraction, and withdrawal of a knowledge attribution must be separated. MacFarlane explicitly distinguishes a sufficient condition for permissible rejection from a sufficient condition for obligatory retraction. Even regarding retraction as a special case of rejection does not turn permission into obligation. Observing an audience’s rejection, or explaining a speaker’s willingness to withdraw, accordingly leaves a further normative question. [2, printed p. 111; PDF p. 125]

Fourth, worldly information and a later assessor’s information cannot be identified without an argument. Beddor and Goldstein discuss an interpretation using provisionally revised conversational or assessor credences, but the dossier correctly distinguishes that option from replacing a use-context parameter with a later assessment parameter. A proposed T2 proof must say whose belief is being tested, which information determines safety, and why that evaluation bears on this speaker’s duty about this earlier assertion. Otherwise two formally similar expressions may concern different subjects. [1, PDF pp. 28–29; 14, T5]

These distinctions constrain criticism as well as defense. MacFarlane’s allowance that one may retract something still thought true does not, by itself, refute a thesis about obligatory retraction. Nor may Hawke’s criticisms of a safety variant without a truth conjunct be transferred without qualification to Beddor and Goldstein’s original formulation. Hawke explicitly announces that alteration in the appendix he tests. [2, printed p. 109; 4, PDF p. 29]

The rivals matter because a superficial bridge can repeat an existing explanation. Dinges develops an asymmetric-loss account of knowledge-attribution judgments and explicitly extends the relevant epistemic qualities to safety and reliability. That is precedent for an explanatory connection involving safety; it does not already establish the requested obligation to retract a bare might-assertion. [5, PDF pp. 10–11]

Hawke’s stable-acceptance account provides conditions for denying knowledge when available evidence fails to support the content. It also leaves questions about the contextual treatment of availability open. Fragmented and topic-sensitive alternatives supply a different account of modal knowledge. Neither simply mentioning an earlier assertion nor adding a retraction predicate establishes a justified extension of those theories. [3, PDF pp. 19, 22; 4, PDF pp. 29–32; 11; 14, T4, T9]

Other proposals relocate the explanation. Beddor and Egan distinguish flexible truth-value assessment from current-context acceptance and retraction. Rudin distinguishes rejection from judgments of falsity. Caponetto analyzes retraction through the normative effects of speech acts, while Bussière-Caraes and colleagues distinguish a proposal to undo effects from its successful uptake. Ball’s temporal externalism allows later conduct to help determine earlier content. These positions make the question more precise: what does worldly-information safety add that the already available pragmatic or metasemantic account does not? [6, PDF pp. 17–18; 7, PDF pp. 7–9; 8, §§2–3; 9, §§7.3–7.4; 10, §§2–3]

The dossier’s not-found verdict for T2 must be applied narrowly. Its necessity-only probe is a partial match to the biconditional. Its sufficiency-only probe is closest to T1 and is partially anticipated. A negation of a T2-related target does not inherit literature support merely because the positive thesis was not located. Further, accepting a safety condition need not entail contextualism, as Ask Zaar argues; Holliday and Mandelkern explicitly identify truth, correctness and retraction as further questions for their framework. These are reasons to state each connection, not to treat the entire surrounding subject as untouched. [12, PDF pp. 1, 10–11; 13, PDF p. 58; 14; 15]

The experiments considered here reused the authenticated gap1r freeze: 160 commitments, comprising 159 source commitments from 22 of 26 input papers and one model-alignment bridge. ## 5. Why sufficiency and necessity need different premises

### 5.1 Sufficiency: from unsafe belief to a duty about an assertion

A compact reconstruction exposes the first missing step. Let K mean that the relevant belief is knowledge, S that it satisfies the relevant safety condition, F that the earlier assertion is not true as used originally and assessed later, and R that the speaker is required to retract that assertion. These are explanatory abbreviations, not ARA’s actual formalization or a replacement semantics for the cited authors.

The safety constraint gives a necessary condition, K → S. Even accepting its classical contrapositive, ¬S → ¬K, yields loss of knowledge rather than F or R. MacFarlane’s retraction rule supplies F → R for the appropriately specified assertion and contexts. Combining the two rules leaves the path from ¬S to F, or directly from ¬S to R, unfilled. A source-supported argument must supply that path; co-citing the two rules does not do so. [1, PDF p. 5; 2, printed p. 108]

A proponent could attempt a truth-mediated bridge: prove that the relevant safety failure makes the very same earlier assertion untrue from the specified assessment context, then apply the Retraction Rule. Alternatively, the proponent could defend an additional epistemic norm that requires retraction without that falsity claim. These are alternatives for investigation. Neither is a premise established by this paper, and the second would require explaining how the proposed duty relates to the assessment-sensitive theory rather than quietly replacing its trigger.

In the old frozen T, even the complete general Retraction Rule was missing. C nominated MacFarlane’s n35 for the biconditional and a later supervaluationism example for the directional targets. The latter is not a general safety-to-retraction rule; nominating it as a basis neither added a premise nor established its use in a licensed proof. The exact general rule is present in the rich extraction as n27 at physical PDF page 122. That distinction explains a repair opportunity without pretending that the repair completes the bridge. [15, 16]

### 5.2 Necessity: the exhaustiveness problem

The converse demands a different kind of justification. T2 requires R → ¬S, or, classically, that safety preservation exclude the relevant obligation. F → R does not license its reversal. Even establishing ¬S → R would provide only sufficiency. A proof of necessity must either derive every relevant obligation’s dependence on safety failure directly or show that an exhaustive account of its grounds entails that dependence.

The word “exhaustive” has substantive work to do. A norm that supplies one sufficient trigger leaves open whether other grounds generate the same obligation. Conversely, showing that a particular trigger fails does not show that no duty remains. T2’s defender must specify the class of obligations being quantified over—constitutive assertoric duties, moral duties, or another restricted class—and justify the exclusion of alternatives within that class. Narrowing the class may yield a more defensible thesis, but it must be disclosed as a change of scope rather than credited as the original biconditional.

Two schematic valuations illustrate only the logical incompleteness of the displayed rules. With K false, S false, F false and R false, both K → S and F → R hold, while ¬S → R fails. With K false, S true, F false and R true, both rules again hold, while R → ¬S fails. These are truth-table witnesses about that small propositional skeleton, not models of the full philosophical theories, realistic cases, or ARA-produced countermodels. Their limited point is that the omitted links cannot be obtained from the two displayed implications alone.

{{fig:missing-bridge}}

MacFarlane’s discussion of permissible retraction of something still thought true must not be used as a shortcut here. Permission and obligation differ. Likewise, a psychological account of why speakers retract does not establish the exhaustiveness of a constitutive norm. A future argument for necessity must engage those distinctions rather than infer a universal rule from a familiar example. [2, printed pp. 109, 111; 5, PDF pp. 10–11]

### 5.3 What the unsuccessful search warrants

The recorded searches support the claim that ARA did not license the supplied statements from this freeze within the tested procedure. The source inspection and the explicit rule structure support the missing-premise diagnosis. These are related but distinct grounds. Since C used whole-statement atoms and bounded search, its OPEN result alone cannot identify every possible missing premise or establish that the full literature entails neither polarity.

This also explains why calling T2 “novel” would overstate the evidence. A target can be absent from a bounded literature search, absent as a decomposed formula in T, and unsupported by a successful proof. Those absences do not combine into a positive contribution. At most they specify a research task whose completion would require an argument, faithful source interpretation and renewed comparison with the literature. [14, 15]

## 7. What a defensible result would need

A positive T2 result would have to preserve a common speaker, belief, assertion token and pair of contexts across its premises. It would need a supported route from safety failure to the relevant obligation and an independently justified route back, with an explicit account of why the obligation has no safety-preserving exception within the stated domain. The formal representation would need to expose those directions instead of merely naming the entire thesis as an atom. Its source-linked proof and remaining critical questions would then need substantive review.

A weaker successful result could still matter. It might establish a suitably restricted sufficient condition, identify a counterexample to one direction, or show that a pragmatic rival handles the target cases without the proposed bridge. Such a result would need its own literature comparison; the dossier already anticipates several tempting reformulations. Labeling that outcome accurately would be more informative than treating every admitted target or polished essay as evidence for the exact biconditional.

The present outcome is narrower. ARA exposed a gap between selected source commitments and a proposed norm, and its focused test did not close it. The literature dossier shows both existing anticipations and a bounded failure to locate the exact T2 defense. This analysis explains the remaining obligations without fulfilling them by stipulation. Gap2 may improve the evidence available to the search; until its results and their supporting inferences are recorded, T2 remains an unlicensed external hypothesis in this experiment.

Epistemic Modals and Information-State-Sensitive Evaluation

The Demand on an Adequate Account

The demand is that an adequate semantics of epistemic modals make modal evaluation sensitive to information states, and not only to worlds. [[node:n0]] This is a claim about what an adequate account must take into consideration: information states are required for modal evaluation, rather than a world-only basis being sufficient. The claim does not say that worlds are irrelevant; it says that evaluation must be sensitive to information states as well. It is a demand on the account, not a claim here that the account has been independently established.

The proposal is supported by two stated commitments. Dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and treats the might-operator as a consistency test. [[node:n1]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] The associated order contrast is that after information that it is not raining is added, that information is no longer consistent with information that it might be raining, while in the reverse order there is no problem. Accordingly, order matters in discourse. [[node:n2]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] These are the grounds for the demand, rather than additional conclusions about every epistemic modal.

The relevance of the commitments can be stated in their exact terms. If conjunction updates information sequentially and “might” tests consistency, then changing the sequence in which information is added changes whether the might-claim remains consistent. The contrast is therefore presented as evidence that epistemic-modal evaluation is information-state-sensitive: the consistency test is applied against an information state, and the sequential interpretation makes order matter to that state. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

On those commitments and that contrast, the account is required to make modal evaluation sensitive to information states, not only to worlds. [[node:n0]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] The point is not merely that the discourse contains different sentences in different orders; the stated support concerns how sequential addition affects consistency of the might-claim. Thus information-state sensitivity is part of the demand because it captures the supplied order effect. This states the direction of support: the commitments and the example support the requirement. It does not assert that the requirement proves the commitments independently, or that the example establishes a universal account of all epistemic modals.

The scope of the demand remains the one expressed in the conclusion. An adequate account must allow information states to matter to modal evaluation, while evaluation by worlds alone is not enough. The supplied support illustrates that demand through sequential conjunction, consistency testing, and the contrast between the two orders of the raining information. The claim is therefore a requirement for adequacy, supported by those commitments and that contrast, rather than a broader claim about all possible modal expressions or a denial that worlds have any role. The example supports the proposal; it does not by itself establish that every epistemic modal behaves in this way. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The demand concerns what an adequate semantics must include in evaluating epistemic modals. Its stated requirement is sensitivity to information states, not exclusive reliance on worlds. [[node:n0]] In this sense, “not only to worlds” preserves a role for worlds while requiring information states to matter too. The claim is about adequacy: it specifies a condition the account must meet, rather than reporting that this condition has been independently established. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The proposal’s support stays tied to the two commitments and the order contrast already stated. Dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and treats might as a consistency test. [[node:n1]] The example describes a particular difference between orders: after information that it is not raining is added, it is no longer consistent with information that it might be raining, while the opposite order presents no problem. [[node:n2]] The demand follows as the proposal supported by these commitments and this contrast, not as a claim that the example settles the behavior of every epistemic modal. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The connection relevant to the demand is that a consistency test is evaluated in relation to information, while the specified interpretation of conjunction is sequential. In the example, the result described for the might-information differs according to whether the not-raining information has first been added or the order is reversed. These are the terms in which the contrast bears on evaluation: it concerns consistency following the addition of information, rather than an order-free description of the two items. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

Accordingly, if the supplied commitments and contrast are taken as the support, the proposed adequacy condition is that modal evaluation must be sensitive to information states. [[node:n0]] The conclusion does not replace information states with worlds, or claim that worlds have no role. It denies only that the demand can be stated as world-only evaluation: the information-state dimension is required by the conclusion as given. This restatement preserves the direction of support, from the commitments and the described order effect to the requirement on an adequate account. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The example’s stated role and limit belong together. It supports the proposal’s claim because the sequential treatment and consistency test explain why the specified order difference matters to modal evaluation. Yet the contrast does not by itself establish that every epistemic modal behaves in that way. The conclusion therefore remains the narrower demand on an adequate semantics, supported by this example and the stated commitments. It should not be recast as a universal claim about every epistemic modal, or as an independently established fact. Sequential Conjunction and Consistency Testing

The semantic commitments at issue are that dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and interprets the might-operator as a consistency test. [[node:n1]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] These are two connected features of the account: conjunction is interpreted in sequence, and might is evaluated by testing consistency. Together, they frame the evaluation of epistemic-modal claims in relation to information states.

To say that conjunction has a sequential interpretation is to retain the order in which its conjuncts are interpreted. The account does not treat conjunction merely as a single undifferentiated unit: its interpretation proceeds sequentially. This commitment specifies how conjunction is handled, without requiring any further claim about the consequences of a particular ordering. It supplies the sequential part of the proposal as stated. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

The second commitment is that the might-operator tests consistency. On this account, evaluating a might-claim involves asking whether it is consistent with the information state against which it is assessed. The operator’s role is thus stated in terms of a consistency test, rather than as a claim that might has some other function. This gives the modal evaluation an explicit relation to information: the relevant question is whether the might-claim remains consistent with the information state. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

The two commitments can be set out separately while keeping their joint role in view. Sequential interpretation concerns how conjunction is interpreted; consistency testing concerns how might is interpreted. The first specifies a sequence in the treatment of conjunction, while the second specifies a test applied in evaluating a modal claim. Neither commitment needs to be enlarged to state the combination: the account gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and treats might as a consistency test. [[node:n1]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

That combination frames epistemic-modal evaluation against information states. Because might is a consistency test, the information state is relevant to the question of whether a might-claim is consistent. Because conjunction is interpreted sequentially, its interpretation preserves a sequence rather than collapsing that feature. The account therefore supplies both an information-related test and a sequential treatment of conjunction. These are the semantic commitments being explained here, not additional conclusions about every modal expression or about other properties of conjunction. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

In this framing, the consistency test is not detached from the information against which the modal claim is evaluated. The stated role of might connects its evaluation to whether the claim is consistent with an information state. The sequential interpretation of conjunction, in turn, provides the specified way of treating conjunction in the same account. The two commitments together describe how the account frames epistemic-modal claims: a sequence is part of conjunction’s interpretation, and consistency with information is part of might’s interpretation. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

The scope here remains limited to those supplied commitments. Dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and interprets might as a consistency test. [[node:n1]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] In particular, describing the test as information-state-related makes explicit the stated setting for the evaluation; it does not add a further semantic rule. Nor does the account, as presented in these commitments, specify a broader claim about all epistemic modals. The commitments establish the framework for considering modal evaluation against information states, while leaving their formulation at the stated level: sequential conjunction and consistency-testing might.

The sequential interpretation and consistency test describe distinct parts of the account. Sequentiality concerns conjunction: its interpretation proceeds in sequence. Consistency testing concerns the might-operator: its interpretation involves testing consistency. Keeping those roles distinct makes clear what each commitment contributes, without adding a further semantic rule or assigning either feature to a different operator. Together, they state how dynamic semantics treats conjunction and might. [[node:n1]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

The first commitment can be restated at the level supplied: conjunction receives a sequential interpretation. This restatement does not specify additional stages, outcomes, or rules for how any particular conjunct changes an information state. Its content is simply that conjunction is interpreted sequentially. That is the first part of the stated semantic pairing. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

The second commitment specifies might in terms of a consistency test. The relevant characterization is not simply that the operator is modal, but that its interpretation tests consistency. This makes consistency the stated feature of might’s interpretation. Put alongside sequential conjunction, it gives the account two coordinated descriptions: one for how conjunction is interpreted, and one for what the might-operator tests. Neither description needs an additional claim about other modal operators. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

These commitments frame epistemic-modal evaluation in relation to information states because the test is one of consistency, and the account identifies the might-operator as the operator that performs it. The pairing therefore keeps both sequence and consistency in view: conjunction is treated sequentially, while might is treated as a consistency test. This explains the stated framing without extending it to a rule about every epistemic modal or to further consequences beyond the two commitments. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

Taken together, the proposal’s content remains exactly these two semantic commitments. The Not-Raining Order Effect

The order effect concerns the order in which information is added in discourse. In one order, information that it is not raining is added before the information that it might be raining. After the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with information that it might be raining. The supplied contrast is therefore not simply between the two pieces of information considered without regard to sequence: it describes what happens when the information is added in one order. [[node:n2]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The other order reverses that sequence. In that opposite order, there is no problem. This contrast matters because the stated outcome differs with the order of addition: adding the not-raining information first leaves it no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining, whereas in the reverse order the sequence presents no problem. The point is the difference between these two discourse orders, not a claim that the two orders have the same result. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The first part of the contrast can be stated narrowly. Information that it is not raining is added; after that addition, it is no longer consistent with information that it might be raining. The claim concerns the relation between the information after the update and the might-information. It does not require replacing the stated description with a broader conclusion about every possible order, every kind of information, or every epistemic modal. The example is specifically about the not-raining information and the information that it might be raining. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The reverse-order part should likewise be kept to its stated scope. When the order is opposite, there is no problem. That is the reported contrast with the sequence in which the not-raining information is added first. The claim does not say more about what happens in the reverse order than that the sequence presents no problem; its role here is to mark the difference between the two orders. In this way, the example makes discourse order explicit without extending the contrast beyond the information supplied. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

Taken together, the two clauses give the order effect its shape: adding the information that it is not raining makes it no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining, while the opposite order presents no problem. The contrast is about the sequence of adding information in discourse. The example’s point can thus be restated without broadening it: in the specified pair of orders, whether the sequence presents a problem varies with which information comes first. [[node:n2]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

This is why the stated conclusion is that order matters in discourse. The first order and the opposite order are not interchangeable in the description: one has the stated loss of consistency after not-raining information is added, and the other has no problem. “Order matters” names that contrast; it does not add a further outcome or a general rule beyond it. The claim remains tied to these two pieces of information and their two orders. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The example therefore focuses on a specific sequence-sensitive contrast. It does not merely state that both not-raining information and might-be-raining information occur in discourse. It distinguishes what follows when the not-raining information is added first from what is said of the opposite order. In the first case, it is no longer consistent with the might-information; in the second, there is no problem. These are the supplied terms of the order effect, and the conclusion that order matters stays within them. [[node:n2]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The contrast can be separated into its two stated orders. In the first, the information that it is not raining is added before the information that it might be raining. After the not-raining information has been added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information. The description therefore attaches the loss of consistency to that specified sequence: first the information that it is not raining, then the information that it might be raining. It does not state that the same result occurs without regard to which information is added first. [[node:n2]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The opposite order supplies the other side of the contrast. When the order is reversed, there is no problem. This clause does not specify some further outcome for that sequence; it says that the reverse order presents no problem. Set beside the first sequence, it makes the difference clear: the not-raining-first order has the stated consistency issue, while the opposite order does not. Both clauses are needed to express the supplied contrast between orders, without making either clause broader than its stated scope. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The phrase “order matters in discourse” summarizes this particular difference. It connects the reported consistency issue with the order in which the two pieces of information are added. It does not introduce another example or claim a result for other information, other sequences, or other modal expressions. Its point here is the stated pair: adding the not-raining information first leaves it no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining; in the reverse order, there is no problem. Thus, the order effect is described through the contrasting results of these two sequences. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

Keeping the parts together preserves the proposition’s full content. It is not merely that one sequence has a problem, since the proposition also specifies what happens in the opposite order. Nor is it merely that the order changes, since the proposition states the associated consistency contrast. The first sequence and its result, the reversed sequence and its result, and the summary that order matters in discourse belong together. Restated narrowly, the supplied claim is that this contrast in adding the two specified pieces of information makes order matter in this discourse example. [[node:n2]] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

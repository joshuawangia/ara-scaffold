# Epistemic Modals and Information-State-Sensitive Evaluation

## Abstract

This paper defends a requirement on the semantics of epistemic modals: an adequate account must make modal evaluation sensitive to information states, not only to worlds. The support comes from two commitments of dynamic semantics, that conjunction receives a sequential interpretation and that the might-operator is interpreted as a consistency test [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj], together with an order contrast: after the information that it is not raining is added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining, whereas in the opposite order there is no problem [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]. Because might tests consistency against an information state, and adding information in a different sequence changes whether the might-claim remains consistent, the contrast counts as evidence for information-state-sensitive evaluation rather than evaluation by worlds alone. The conclusion is a demand on an adequate semantics, not a claim that worlds play no role and not an independently established result. Its limit is equally explicit: the example supports the proposal but does not by itself establish that every epistemic modal behaves this way. The paper sets out these components, answers objections and marks the limits of the support.

## 1. Introduction: The Demand on an Adequate Account

The demand defended is that an adequate semantics of epistemic modals must make modal evaluation sensitive to information states, not only to worlds.  The conclusion thus specifies what an adequate account must take into consideration: information states, and not worlds alone, must matter to modal evaluation. The demand is not for world-only evaluation; it requires that modal evaluation also be sensitive to information states. It is a demand placed on the account, not a claim, made here, that such an account has been independently established.

The proposal rests on two stated commitments: dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation, and it treats the might-operator as a consistency test.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] The order contrast adds that after the information that it is not raining is added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining, whereas in the opposite order no problem arises. Order, accordingly, matters in discourse.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] These are the grounds for the demand, not further conclusions about every epistemic modal.

The relevance of these commitments can be stated in their own terms. Together with the order contrast, they support the conclusion: might tests consistency on an information state, and adding information in a different sequence changes whether the might-claim remains consistent. The contrast is therefore offered as evidence that epistemic-modal evaluation is information-state-sensitive: the consistency test is applied against an information state, and the sequential interpretation makes order matter to that state. The supporting commitments and contrast are sequential conjunction, consistency testing, and the difference between the two orders in which the raining information is added. ## 3. Argument Continued: The Not-Raining Order Effect

The order effect at issue concerns the sequence in which information is added in discourse. In the order described first, the information that it is not raining is added before the information that it might be raining. The reported result is that, once the information that it is not raining has been added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining. The contrast is therefore not simply between two pieces of information considered apart from their sequence. Rather, it describes what happens when the information is added in one particular order.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The other side of the contrast is the opposite order. Of that order, the description says only that there is no problem. The reported outcome therefore varies with the order of addition: after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information, whereas in the reverse order there is no problem. The point is the difference between these two discourse orders. Nothing here claims that the two orders have the same result. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The first half of the contrast can be stated narrowly. Its result is that, after the information that it is not raining has been added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining. The contrast thus concerns whether the might-information is still consistent once the not-raining information has been added. It does not require replacing this description with a broader conclusion about every possible order, every kind of information, or every epistemic modal. The example concerns only the not-raining information and the information that it might be raining. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The reverse-order half should likewise be kept to its stated scope. The description of the opposite order is simply that there is no problem. That is the reported contrast with the order in which the not-raining information is added first. About the reverse order, the claim says only that the sequence presents no problem; its role here is to mark the difference between the two orders. In this way, the example makes discourse order explicit without extending the contrast beyond the information given. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

Taken together, the two stated outcomes are these: after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information, while in the opposite order there is no problem. The contrast concerns the sequence in which information is added in discourse. The example's point can therefore be restated without broadening it: for the specified pair of orders, whether the sequence presents a problem varies with which information comes first.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The conclusion drawn from this contrast is that order matters in discourse. The two orders have different reported outcomes: after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information; in the opposite order, there is no problem. "Order matters" names that contrast. It adds no further outcome and no general rule. The claim remains tied to these two pieces of information and their two orders. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The example, then, states a specific contrast between two orders in which the not-raining information and the might-be-raining information can be added. It distinguishes the order in which the not-raining information comes first from the opposite order. The reported outcomes are that, after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information, and that in the opposite order there is no problem. These are the terms of the order effect, and the conclusion that order matters does not go beyond them.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The contrast can also be broken down into its two stated orders. In the first order, the information that it is not raining is added before the information that it might be raining. The reported result is that, after the information that it is not raining has been added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining. The contrast assigns this consistency result to the not-raining-first order and sets it against the opposite order. It does not say that the same result holds whichever piece of information is added first.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The opposite order supplies the other side of the contrast. Of that order, the description says that there is no problem. This clause gives no further outcome for the sequence; it says only that the reverse order presents no problem. The full contrast is thus that, after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information, whereas in the opposite order there is no problem. Both clauses are needed to state the contrast between the orders, and neither should be read more broadly than its stated scope allows. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The claim sums up this particular contrast by saying that order matters in discourse. The contrast concerns the order in which the not-raining information and the might-information are added. It introduces no further example and claims no result for other information, other sequences, or other modal expressions. The stated pair is that, after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information, and that in the reverse order there is no problem. The order effect is thus described through the different results of these two sequences. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

Keeping the two parts together preserves the full content of the proposition. The contrast specifies both outcomes: after the not-raining information is added, it is no longer consistent with the might-information; in the opposite order, there is no problem. It states that order matters in discourse because adding the not-raining information first produces a consistency problem, while the opposite order produces none. In this way, it gives the result for each order and sums up the pair by saying that order matters in discourse. Stated narrowly, the claim is that this contrast between the two orders of adding the specified information makes order matter in this discourse example.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

## 5. Force of the Support

The central conclusion is that an adequate semantics of epistemic modals must make modal evaluation sensitive to information states, not only to worlds.  Its stated support combines two commitments, one about conjunction and one about might, with a contrast between two orders of adding information. Dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and treats the might-operator as a consistency test.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

The contrast is specific. As stated, after the information that it is not raining is added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining; in the opposite order, there is no problem. The contrast is summed up in the claim that order matters in discourse.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] The support relies on this difference between the specified sequences and makes no claim about every possible order or every modal.

These two commitments explain why the difference bears on modal evaluation. Conjunction receives a sequential interpretation, and might is treated as a consistency test. Under these commitments, the question for a might-claim is whether it remains consistent with the information state against which it is assessed. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] When the sequence of information changes, the consistency outcome in the stated example changes too: adding the not-raining information first leaves it no longer consistent with the might-information, whereas the reverse order presents no problem. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The example therefore supports information-state-sensitive evaluation: sequential addition affects the information state, and the might-test checks consistency with that state. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] This is the direction of support. Given the stated commitments and the order contrast, an adequate semantics must make modal evaluation sensitive to information states rather than relying on worlds alone.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

## 6. Objections and Replies

One objection is that a single order effect is too narrow to bear on epistemic modals in general. The reply is that the scope of the conclusion should be stated exactly. What it demands is sensitivity to information states, not only to worlds. The particular order effect does not by itself establish that every epistemic modal behaves this way, and the conclusion does not claim that it does. The example supports the proposal's claim, but only in a limited role: it illustrates how consistency testing against sequentially updated information makes order relevant to modal evaluation. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

A second objection is that the argument simply assumes what it needs. In reply, the claim is explicitly conditional on the stated premises and on the connection drawn between them. If conjunction is interpreted sequentially, might tests consistency, and the described order contrast holds, then the contrast supports the demand that an adequate semantics make modal evaluation sensitive to information states, not only to worlds.    [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] The conclusion remains a requirement supported by these commitments and this example, not an independently established universal claim about epistemic modals.

This limit on the example's support determines how the conclusion should be stated. The order contrast supports information-state-sensitive modal evaluation: the might-claim is tested for consistency with information added in sequence. Yet the example does not, by itself, establish that every epistemic modal behaves this way. The conclusion is therefore the stated demand on an adequate semantics, not a universal generalization about all epistemic modals.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj]

This distinction keeps both sides of the support in view. On one side, the example's stated contrast is that after the information that it is not raining is added, it is no longer consistent with the information that it might be raining, while in the opposite order there is no problem. This is the specified sense in which order matters in discourse.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order] On the other side, the contrast is not stretched beyond its stated role. It shows how the specified sequences differ, but it does not establish that every epistemic modal behaves the same way. To the question whether the contrast is reliable evidence, the answer is thus yes: the example counts as evidence for information-state sensitivity, subject to the stated limit on what that evidence establishes. [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

## 7. Limits

The same care applies to the semantic commitments. Dynamic semantics gives conjunction a sequential interpretation and interprets the might-operator as a consistency test.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] The commitments and the order contrast support the conclusion that modal evaluation must be sensitive to information states, not only to worlds. They do not turn the example into a claim about every modal, and the conclusion does not need that wider claim in order to state its demand. What the conclusion says is that an adequate semantics of epistemic modals must make information states matter to evaluation.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

The relevant inference therefore remains conditional on the stated commitments and contrast. If these are taken as support, they support information-state-sensitive evaluation, not evaluation by worlds alone. Nothing here claims that the premises have been independently established, and no conclusion is drawn about other sequences or modalities. The inference restates the direction of the support provided: it runs from the commitments and the specified order effect to the requirement on an adequate semantics.    [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]

## 8. Conclusion

In sum, the conclusion requires modal evaluation to be sensitive to information states, not only to worlds, and the stated commitments and order contrast support information-state-sensitive modal evaluation. The limit is just as precise: this support does not by itself establish that every epistemic modal behaves the same way. Stating the requirement and its limit together keeps the conclusion's scope intact. The support licenses the demand for information-state-sensitive evaluation, but it does not license a universal claim about all epistemic modals.  [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_seq_conj] [cite:gsv1996:r18-35:n_order]
